=== SLIDE 8: PROMPT DESIGN ===
Title: Prompt design: ISO-ScreenPrompt
Intro line:
  We adopt the prompt structure Cao et al. built for screening long full-text
  articles. ISO stands for Instruction Structure Optimized.
Bullets:
  - Framework chain-of-thought: the model reasons against our numbered
    criteria one at a time, rather than reasoning freely.
  - Instruction structure optimized: the promptbook is repeated before and
    after the article text, because models retrieve well at the start and end
    of a long context and poorly in the middle.
  - Every sub-criterion is numbered, which pushes the model to reason against
    the specific rule rather than a vague overall impression.
  - Zero-shot: no worked examples. Every response returns four fields: the
    decision, the reasoning, the promptbook rule relied on, and a confidence.
Table showing why each piece earns its place (Cao, abstract screening, GPT-4):
  Prompt                                    Accuracy   Sensitivity
  Bare zero-shot                               65.0%         30.0%
  Random few-shot                              78.0%         56.0%
  Zero-shot CoT ("think step by step")         86.3%         74.5%
  Framework CoT                                91.3%         86.5%
  Full ScreenPrompt                            94.3%         94.5%
  ScreenPrompt plus few-shot examples          85.8%         73.5%
Callout, sienna border, titled "Why no examples in the prompt":
  Adding labelled examples made things worse, dropping accuracy from 94.3% to
  85.8%. Cao calls this semantic contamination: the model treats the reasoning
  in the examples as directly relevant to the paper in front of it, and starts
  pattern-matching to the examples instead of applying the criteria. We give
  the model rules, not examples.
Source: Cao C et al. Prompting is all you need: LLMs for systematic review
screening. medRxiv 2024. doi:10.1101/2024.06.01.24308323


=== SLIDE 9: WHICH MODEL ===
Title: Which model, and when
Two-stage diagram, left to right:
  SONNET (first pass, all papers)  ->  confident? -> keep the answer
                                   ->  low confidence or undecidable? ->
  OPUS (second pass)               ->  final answer

=== SLIDE 11: THE API DOES NOT REMEMBER ===
Title: The API does not remember, and that matters
Bullets:
  - Every API call is stateless. The model receives the prompt and the paper,
    returns an answer, and retains nothing.
  - Calling it again on the same paper does not build on the previous call. It
    starts from the same blank state.
  - This is not fine-tuning. We never change the model's weights. We change the
    promptbook, which is a document we control and version in git.
Callout, forest border, titled "Why this is useful":
  Any paper can be re-run safely at any time. Re-running does not contaminate
  the result, does not leak the previous answer, and does not teach the model
  anything. An interrupted batch can simply be replayed. Self-consistency
  voting works for exactly this reason: three calls on one paper are three
  genuinely independent opinions, not one opinion repeated.
Small note, muted:
  This is also why the holdout stays clean. A paper seen during promptbook
  development leaves no trace in the model, only in the promptbook a human
  wrote afterwards.


=== SLIDE 12: SELF-CONSISTENCY VOTING ===
Title: Self-consistency: is it worth asking more than once?
Intro line:
  Ask the model the same question several times and take the majority answer.
  Cao et al. used 11 votes, on the Batch API.
Bullets:
  - Model answers vary between runs. On a borderline paper a single call is
    close to a coin flip; a majority vote is stabler and reproducible.
  - Cost is linear in the number of votes.
What it actually bought Cao, on full-text screening with ISO-ScreenPrompt:
  Dataset        Accuracy       Sensitivity     Specificity     Cost
  SeroTracker    96.5 to 97.5   98.5 to 97.5    94.5 to 97.5    $51 to $142
  Reinfection    83.3 to 87.5   89.7 to 92.4    79.6 to 84.7    $51 to $131
Our own projected cost, highlight the 3-vote row:
  Votes   Calls   Input tokens   Approx. cost (batch)
      1    2.2k          ~33M                    $58
      3    6.6k         ~100M                   $174
     11   24.2k         ~364M                   $638
Two closing callouts side by side:
  RECOMMENDATION (petrol): Three votes. Cao's 11 votes bought 1 to 4 points of
  accuracy for roughly 2.6 times the cost, and most of that gain arrives well
  before the eleventh vote. Their largest gains were in specificity, on their
  worst-performing dataset.
  THE TUNABLE KNOB (forest): The vote threshold is a dial, not just a majority.
  Cao swept it from 0 to 12 votes to trade sensitivity against specificity and
  drew an ROC curve from it. We can tune where our gate sits on that curve.
Small note, muted:
  Cao varied the random seed per vote. Claude does not expose a seed or a
  temperature setting, so our votes vary through the model's own stochasticity.
Source: Cao C et al. medRxiv 2024. doi:10.1101/2024.06.01.24308323,
Supplementary Table 17. Self-consistency: Wang X et al. arXiv:2203.11171, 2022.


=== SLIDE 13: WHY NOT BERT ===
Title: Why a language model, and not a classifier
Four bullets with bold lead-ins:
  - No fine-tuning, no training data. BERT-style classifiers need thousands of
    labelled examples. We have 176 for power analysis, and training on them
    would consume the very set we need as the Human Labelled Set.
  - It explains itself. Every judgment comes back with reasoning and the rule
    it relied on, so a mistake is diagnosable. A classifier returns a number
    and no argument.
  - We iterate in English, not in weights. Fixing an error means editing a
    sentence in a versioned document, not retraining a model.
  - Cost is no longer the obstacle. Roughly 1,300 calls for the gate; a
    fine-tuning pipeline would cost far more in researcher time than this
    costs in API spend.
Closing line, muted:
  The deciding factor is the second one. This study has to be auditable.
Source: Devlin J et al. BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers
for Language Understanding. arXiv:1810.04805, 2019.


=== SLIDE 14: STAGE 4, THE PROMPTBOOK LOOP ===
Title: Stage 4. The promptbook loop
Give the diagram most of the slide. Cycle of five stages, first three petrol,
last two plum:
  Sample a batch -> Model classifies -> Score against the human answer ->
  Triage the misses -> Update the promptbook -> (back to Sample)
Bullets, short:
  - One task at a time, in order: exclusion, then power, then data. Never in
    parallel.
  - Each round samples a batch from the build split, classifies it, and scores
    it against the expert answers.
  - A miss becomes a rule only when we see it at least twice. A one-off goes to
    a person to read, because it may be a bad label rather than a bad rule.
  - No promptbook change is committed without human approval, and every round
    is recorded: the judgments, the promptbook version, and the accuracy delta.
  - Stop when two consecutive rounds each gain less than one percentage point.

=== SLIDE 17: HOW WE MEASURE IT ===
Title: How we measure it, and why accuracy alone would mislead you
Opening line, set large:
  A model that answers "incorrect" to every power analysis scores 77%.
Table:
  Task         n     Class split                  Always guess majority
  Exclusion  492     316 exclude / 176 keep                        64%
  Power      176     136 incorrect / 40 correct                    77%
  Data       176     129 incorrect / 47 correct                    73%
Second table, Zupic's three-way framework for judging LLM output:
  Question              Metrics we report
  Validity              Accuracy, precision, recall, F1, sensitivity, specificity
  Reliability           Cohen's kappa, intraclass correlation across repeat runs
  Robustness            Agreement across models (Sonnet versus Opus)
Bullets:
  - Cohen's kappa is agreement corrected for luck. It asks how much better than
    chance two raters agree, which matters when one answer dominates.
  - Zupic files kappa under reliability, not validity, and uses it for both
    inter-rater and inter-model agreement. We use it for both.
  - We are comparing a model to human raters, not to truth, so kappa is the
    right headline and raw accuracy is not.
  - The two institutes agreed with each other on 12 of 15 shared papers. That
    80% is the bar, not perfection.
Callout, sienna:
  For exclusion the errors are not symmetric. A wrong "keep" is caught
  downstream by a human. A wrong "exclude" removes the paper permanently.
Source: Zupic I. arXiv:2603.19271, 2026, Table 3.


=== SLIDE 18: WHAT WE HAVE ALREADY FOUND ===
Title: What the accounting already turned up
Three finding cards:
  EVERY PAPER ACCOUNTED FOR
  Twelve papers turned out to be in neither corpus: dropped from one set
  because a copy existed in the other, which was then dropped too. They are
  being restored.

  THIRTY-ONE UNLEARNABLE EXCLUSIONS
  The original protocol used a random number generator to drop one of any two
  papers sharing a first author. No model reading a single PDF can reproduce
  that, so those papers leave the scored set.

  TWO DIFFERENT EXCLUSION PROTOCOLS
  NCI and NHLBI did not apply identical criteria. Raised with the study team;
  source institute will be carried as a covariate.
Closing line, muted:
  None of this was visible until every paper was tracked individually.
